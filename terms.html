4. Recording, consent, and your legal obligations

Jefe records audio while you walk, and keeps recording while your phone is locked or in your pocket until you finish the walk. Recording laws vary by state, and following them is your responsibility.

  • Consent to record. Roughly a dozen states require every person in a conversation to agree before it may be recorded, and in several of them recording without that agreement is a criminal offence rather than merely a civil wrong. Some of those states — Massachusetts is the clearest example — prohibit secret recording regardless of whether anyone had any expectation of privacy, so “we were outdoors” is not a defence there. Elsewhere the risk concentrates where people do expect privacy, which ordinarily includes the inside of a home. The simplest rule, and the one we recommend everywhere: say out loud that you are recording, every time someone else is present.
  • Recording people you are not talking to. If your phone captures a conversation you are not part of — people talking in another room while you work — that can be unlawful under federal and state wiretap law even in states that otherwise allow one-party consent, because those laws require the recorder to be a participant. Stop the walk when you are not the one talking.
  • Licences and registrations. You are responsible for holding whatever licences, registrations, bonds, and insurance your trade and your jurisdiction require.
  • Contract requirements. Many states regulate home improvement contracts, requiring a written agreement, signatures, and specific terms once a job passes a dollar threshold. A document Jefe produces is a draft and is not guaranteed to satisfy those requirements. Making sure the agreement you actually enter into complies is your responsibility.
  • Other people’s information. A walk often captures gate codes, lockbox codes, addresses and client details. Handle that information the way its owner would expect — it isn’t yours, and it can open a door.
